- Raw reports and traces: [# EU Battery Recycling Law: From Directive 2006/66/EC to Regulation (EU) 2023/1542 ## 1. Executive Summary Battery recycling in the EU is now governed by **Regulation (EU) 2023/1542 on batteries and waste batteries**, which replaced the **Batteries Directive 2006/66/EC**. The Regulation entered into force on **17 August 2023** and applies generally from **18 February 2024**, but the old Directive was only **repealed with effect from 18 August 2025** (Article 95), with a handful of its provisions surviving for limited transitional periods until 18 February 2027 and 30 June 2027 [1][2][3]. The replacement was motivated by technological change (lithium-ion, e-mobility, stationary storage), the circular economy and European Green Deal agenda, critical raw material supply security, and the Directive's uneven implementation and outdated design [4][5][6]. The new Regulation introduces a full life-cycle framework: new battery categories, carbon footprint rules, recycled content mandates, a digital battery passport, due diligence, and higher collection and recycling targets [7][8]. Two common misconceptions should be corrected up front: the Directive set a **65%** (not 50%) recycling efficiency target for lead-acid batteries [9][10], and the Directive was **not** repealed on the date the new Regulation started applying (18 February 2024) — repeal took effect on **18 August 2025** [1][2]. --- ## 2. The Previous Instrument: Directive 2006/66/EC ### 2.1 Identity and formal details | Item | Detail | |---|---| | **Full official title** | "Directive 2006/66/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of 6 September 2006 on batteries and accumulators and waste batteries and accumulators and repealing Directive 91/157/EEC" [9][11][12] | | **OJ citation** | OJ L 266, 26.9.2006, pp. 1–14 [9][11][12] | | **Adoption/signature** | 6 September 2006, Strasbourg [9][11] | | **Entry into force** | 26 September 2006 (date of publication, Art. 29) [9][11][12] | | **Transposition deadline** | 26 September 2008 (Art. 26) [9][11][12] | | **Legal basis** | Dual: Art. 175(1) EC Treaty (environment), with Art. 95(1) EC for heavy-metal content and labelling rules [9][11] | | **Predecessor replaced** | Council Directive 91/157/EEC of 18 March 1991, repealed with effect from 26 September 2008 [11][12] | | **Amendments consolidated** | Directives 2008/12/EC, 2008/103/EC, 2013/56/EU, (EU) 2018/849; consolidated version dated 04/07/2018 (CELEX 02006L0066-20180704) [9][11] | The Directive was a **directive**, meaning it required transposition into national law and left Member States latitude over the means of implementation — a feature later identified as a cause of divergent national regimes [4][6]. ### 2.2 Scope Article 2(1) applied the Directive to **all types of batteries and accumulators, regardless of shape, volume, weight, material composition or use**, including batteries incorporated into appliances, and without prejudice to the End-of-Life Vehicles Directive (2000/53/EC) and the WEEE Directive (2002/96/EC) [9][11][13]. The Commission's rationale for an "all-battery" approach was that all batteries contain harmful substances, "all battery" collection schemes are more efficient than separate schemes, and all batteries contain recyclable metals [13]. Exclusions under Article 2(2) covered batteries used in: - equipment connected with the **protection of Member States' essential security interests, arms, munitions and war material** (excluding products not intended for specifically military purposes); - equipment **designed to be sent into space** [9][11][13]. Medical devices were **not** excluded from scope; the only concession was a derogation from the cadmium ban for portable batteries in medical equipment [11][13]. Fuel cells, classical capacitors and super-capacitors fell outside the definition of "battery" [13]. The Directive recognised three battery categories via Article 3: - **Portable battery** — sealed, hand-carried, neither industrial nor automotive [9][11]; - **Automotive battery** — used for starter, lighting or ignition (SLI) power [9][11]; - **Industrial battery** — "any battery or accumulator designed for exclusively industrial or professional uses or used in any type of electric vehicle" [11]. ### 2.3 Key recycling-related obligations #### Separate collection and disposal restrictions - **Article 7** required Member States to maximise separate collection of waste batteries and minimise disposal as unsorted municipal waste [9][11]. - **Article 8(1)** required accessible collection points for portable batteries, free take-back by distributors when supplying new batteries (unless alternative arrangements were demonstrated to be at least as effective), and no charge or purchase obligation on end-users returning waste portable batteries [9][11][13]. - **Article 8(3)** required producers to take back waste industrial batteries from end-users and prohibited them from refusing take-back regardless of chemical composition or origin [9][11][13]. - **Article 8(4)** required producers to set up free collection schemes for waste automotive batteries from private, non-commercial vehicle users [9][11][13]. - **Article 14** prohibited the **landfilling or incineration of waste industrial and automotive batteries**, with an exception only for residues that had undergone both treatment and recycling [9][11][14]. Portable batteries were not subject to an outright disposal ban — only the Article 7 minimisation objective [13]. #### Collection targets — Article 10(2) The Directive set minimum collection rates for **portable batteries only**: - **25% by 26 September 2012**; - **45% by 26 September 2016** [9][11][12][13]. The calculation (Annex I) divided the weight of waste portable batteries collected in a year by the average weight placed on the market in that year and the two preceding years — i.e. **CR = 3 × Cₙ / (S₁ + S₂ + S₃)** [11][12]. The methodology was implemented by Commission Decision 2008/763/EC [11][13]. **No collection targets were set for industrial or automotive batteries**; the Commission reasoned that take-back obligations plus the disposal ban sufficed, and that nearly 100% of these larger, valuable batteries were already collected [13]. #### Recycling efficiency — Article 12(4) and Annex III Part B Minimum recycling efficiencies had to be achieved **by 26 September 2011** [11][12][13]: | Battery type | Minimum recycling efficiency (by average weight) | Additional condition | |---|---|---| | **Lead-acid** | **65%** | Recycle lead as far as technically feasible | | **Nickel-cadmium** | **75%** | Recycle cadmium as far as technically feasible | | **Other waste batteries** | **50%** | — | > **Correction to a common assumption:** the lead-acid figure under the Directive was **65%**, not 50%. The 50% figure applied to "other" batteries [9][11][13]. Calculation rules were laid down by Commission Regulation (EU) No 493/2012 [13]. By 26 September 2009, producers or third parties had to have set up treatment and recycling schemes using best available techniques [9][11][13]. Exports of waste batteries for treatment outside the EU counted toward obligations only where there was sound evidence of broadly equivalent conditions, subject to the Waste Shipments Regulation (EC) No 1013/2006 [9][11][13]. #### Producer responsibility — Articles 16–19 Although the phrase "extended producer responsibility" did not appear in the operative text, the Directive created an EPR-style architecture: - **Article 16(1)**: producers (or third parties on their behalf) must finance net costs of collecting, treating and recycling all waste portable, industrial and automotive batteries [9][11][14]. Net costs are net of revenue from recovered materials [11]. - **Article 16(2)**: no double charging where batteries are collected under ELV or WEEE schemes [9][11]. - **Article 16(3)**: producers must finance public information campaigns on portable battery collection and recycling [9][11][13]. - **Article 16(4)**: costs must not be shown separately to end-users at the point of sale [9][11]. - **Article 16(5)**: producers and users of industrial/automotive batteries may agree financing arrangements [11][13]. - **Article 16(6)**: the financing obligation covered **historic/orphan waste** — batteries placed on the market before the Directive entered into force [9][11]. - **Article 17 and Annex IV**: every producer had to be **registered** in each Member State where it placed batteries on the market; harmonised requirements were originally set by Commission Decision 2009/603/EC and later moved into Annex IV by Directive 2013/56/EU [9][11][13]. - **Article 18**: Member States could exempt producers placing very small quantities on the market, subject to Commission notification and approval within six months [9][11][13]. - **Article 19**: collection and recycling schemes had to be non-discriminatory, including toward imported batteries [9][11]. #### Labelling and removability - **Article 21(1)**: all batteries had to bear the **crossed-out wheeled bin symbol** (Annex II) [9][11][12]. - **Article 21(2)**: capacity marking for portable and automotive batteries by 26 September 2009, implemented by Commission Regulation (EU) No 1103/2010 [11][13]. - **Article 21(3)**: chemical symbols **Hg, Cd, Pb** where thresholds of 0.0005% mercury, 0.002% cadmium or 0.004% lead were exceeded [9][11][13]. - **Article 11**: manufacturers had to design appliances so waste batteries could be **readily removed** by end-users, or by independent qualified professionals where end-user removal was not possible [9][11][14]. --- ## 3. Reasons for Replacement and Key Changes ### 5.1 Why the Directive was replaced The Commission's **proposal COM(2020) 798 final** of 10 December 2020 framed batteries as a strategic imperative and identified three problem groups: 1. **Insufficient framework conditions for sustainable battery production** — inefficient single-market functioning, an uneven playing field, diverging regulatory frameworks, uneven implementation of the Batteries Directive, and a lack of reliable comparable information [4]. 2. **Sub-optimal recycling markets and insufficiently closed material loops** — "unclear, insufficiently harmonised rules and outdated provisions reduce recycling profitability and deter investment" [4]. 3. **Social and environmental risks outside EU environmental law** — lack of transparency on raw-material sourcing and hazardous substances, and untapped potential to offset life-cycle impacts [4]. The evaluation underpinning the proposal (COM(2019) 166 final, April 2019) identified limitations of the Directive in a fundamentally changed market context [4][6]. **Technological and market change.** The Directive predated the lithium-ion era. Global battery demand is forecast to grow sharply — the Commission cited a 14-fold increase by 2030 [17][18], and the EU alone is expected to need 18 times more lithium in 2030 and up to 50 times more in 2050 than in 2018, with cobalt demand five times higher by 2030 [17]. The EU expects at least 30 million zero-emission vehicles by 2030 [18][19]. New product categories — LMT (e-bikes, e-scooters), stationary battery energy storage, EV traction batteries — were not adequately addressed by the Directive's three categories [6][17][18]. **Circular economy and Green Deal.** The Regulation was the first initiative under the **Circular Economy Action Plan of 11 March 2020**, a key component of the European Green Deal [5][17]. The Commission's stated aims were to ensure batteries have a low carbon footprint, use minimal harmful substances, need fewer raw materials from non-EU countries, and are collected, reused and recycled to a high degree within the EU [8]. **Critical raw materials and strategic autonomy.** Battery manufacturing depends heavily on lithium, cobalt, nickel and natural graphite, whose extraction is concentrated in a few non-EU nations, exposing Europe to supply-chain vulnerabilities [5][17]. The Regulation complements the Critical Raw Materials Act and supports EU strategic autonomy [5][18]. **Uneven implementation and the Directive's legal form.** The Directive gave Member States wide interpretive latitude, causing uneven implementation. The Commission chose a **Regulation** precisely to ensure direct, simultaneous and uniform application across all 27 Member States and legal certainty for investment [4][6]. ### 5.2 Key changes — the old rules versus the new rules | Topic | Directive 2006/66/EC | Regulation (EU) 2023/1542 | |---|---|---| | **Legal form** | Directive — required national transposition; The Batteries Directive's Legacy and Transitional Effects The Directive's architecture — separate collection, producer financing, registration, recycling efficiency — survives in substance in the Regulation, but with tighter, directly applicable rules and higher targets [1][7]. The transitional provisions in Article 95 preserve a few Directive rules only briefly: removability until 18 February 2027, recycling efficiency until 31 December 2025 (data transmission until 30 June 2027), and capacity marking until 18 August 2026 [1][2][3]. Member State implementing measures adopted under the Directive should be read as references to the corresponding Regulation provisions [1][2]. The Regulation has already been amended several times: by Regulation (EU) 2024/1781 (ESPR, June 2024), by **Regulation (EU) 2025/1561** (July 2025, postponing due diligence to 18 August 2027), and by Regulation (EU) 2026/1738 (July 2026, vehicle lead/cadmium limits) [7][8][10]. The consolidated version is dated 13 August 2026 [7]. --- ## 7. Conclusion The EU's battery recycling regime has moved from a 2006 directive built around three battery types and modest end-of-life targets to a 2023 regulation that governs the entire battery life cycle. The **Batteries Directive 2006/66/EC** set portable collection targets of 25% (2012) and 45% (2016), recycling efficiencies of 65% lead-acid, 75% NiCd and 50% other by 2011, a landfill/incineration ban for industrial and automotive batteries, and producer-financed collection and registration [9][11][13]. The **Regulation (EU) 2023/1542**, published on 28 July 2023 and in force since 17 August 2023, applies generally from 18 February 2024 and repealed the Directive from **18 August 2025** [1][2][3]. It introduces five battery categories, portable targets rising to 63% (2027) and 73% (2030), LMT targets of 51% (2028) and 61% (2031), higher recycling efficiencies and new material recovery targets, carbon footprint rules, recycled content mandates from 2031, a battery passport and QR code from February 2027, and due diligence from August 2027 [4][5][7][8]. The replacement was driven by technological change, the circular economy and Green Deal agenda, critical raw material security, and the Directive's uneven implementation — addressed by choosing a
